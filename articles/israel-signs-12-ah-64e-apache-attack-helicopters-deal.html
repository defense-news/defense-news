Israel Signs $1 Billion Deal for 12 New AH-64E Apache Attack Helicopters

James CarterOct 18, 2026 12:40 UTC 3 min readRegion: Middle East

JERUSALEM — Israel signed an agreement with the United States on October 14 to buy 12 Boeing AH-64E Apache Guardian attack helicopters, closing a deal worth an estimated $1 billion that nearly collapsed over a domestic budget fight.

The Defense Ministry said Director General Maj. Gen. (res.) Amir Baram instructed Israel's defense mission in Washington to sign the agreement after the cabinet approved it on Wednesday. Deliveries are expected in the early 2030s. The ministry did not disclose the contract's value; Israeli business daily Calcalist estimated it at about $1 billion.

"The execution of the Apache agreement was made possible thanks to creative solutions we developed together with our American partners, without compromising operational needs," Baram said in the ministry's statement. Defense Minister Israel Katz called the purchase "a critical step in the IDF's force buildup, one that will strengthen its operational capabilities."

A deal that nearly slipped away

The purchase stalled for months over a $288 million financing dispute between the Defense and Finance ministries. Washington granted Israel three deadline extensions, and Israel still missed the final ordering deadline the week before the signing. The cabinet then approved shifting about NIS 1 billion into defense procurement — reportedly drawn largely from a 2027-2029 development program for Arab communities, with another NIS 68 million from the 2026 state budget — allowing the original 12-aircraft quantity to survive.

The 12 helicopters represent 40 percent of the package Washington cleared earlier this year. In January, the U.S. State Department approved a possible foreign military sale of up to 30 AH-64Es and related equipment at an estimated ceiling of $3.8 billion. A foreign military sales approval sets a ceiling rather than a contract, and Israel has now signed for the first batch, leaving 18 aircraft outside the agreement.

What the AH-64E brings

The aircraft will be the Version 6 configuration of the Apache, the newest in service. Its mast-mounted AN/APG-78 Longbow fire control radar can detect targets out to 16 kilometers, double the reach of earlier configurations, and track up to 128 targets while prioritizing 16 for engagement.

Version 6 adds maritime targeting functions for detecting and engaging surface vessels, including small craft in coastal waters — capabilities relevant to operations along the Mediterranean coast and near Gaza. The helicopter also fields manned-unmanned teaming, allowing crews to receive data from compatible drones and, depending on the setup, control their sensors or flight functions.

The 12 new aircraft will join an aging fleet: 23 AH-64A Petens acquired starting in 1990 and 20 AH-64D Sarafs delivered from 2005. By 2032, the earliest AH-64As will have served 42 years, sharpening the case for replacements even as Israel debates whether the new aircraft replace or supplement the old.

The Apache order is the latest in a string of major Israeli purchases of American aircraft. In November 2024, Israel signed a $5.2 billion deal for 25 Boeing F-15IA fighters, and in May it approved a plan for a fourth F-35 squadron and a second F-15IA squadron, with those agreements still being finalized.

Why it matters

The drama of this purchase was never really about helicopters — it was about money. Israel is simultaneously financing a multi-year force buildup across fighters, air defenses and munitions, and the $288 million gap that nearly killed the Apache deal is a window into the strain. That the cabinet ultimately redirected a domestic development program's funds to keep the order alive shows how firmly attack aviation ranks in the IDF's priorities — and how thin the fiscal cushion has become.

The capabilities tell their own story. The maritime targeting package and the manned-unmanned teaming suite are not Cold War tank-killing requirements; they are built for the wars Israel is actually fighting — spotting small boats off the coast, pairing gunships with drones to hunt dispersed targets, and coordinating fires with ground troops in dense urban terrain. Israel's Apaches have been flying exactly those missions since October 2023.

What to watch: whether Jerusalem funds the remaining 18 authorized Apaches, and the follow-on agreements Baram flagged for platforms, munitions and interceptors — the weapons that will decide what these helicopters can actually do when they arrive in the 2030s.
