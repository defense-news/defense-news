Senate Democrats Demand Pentagon Disclose the 'True Cost' of the Iran War

David SullivanOct 19, 2026 12:39 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

Five Democratic senators are demanding the Pentagon publicly reveal the full cost of the war in Iran, accusing the Trump administration of hiding how much damage Iranian strikes have done to American bases across the Middle East.

The senators wrote to Adm. Brad Cooper, the head of U.S. Central Command, that the administration's "ongoing efforts to hide the true cost of this war are outrageous and unacceptable." The letter, first reported by USA Today on Monday, asks for the estimated cost of the damage, a timeline for beginning repairs, and how much has been spent adapting new facilities to account for the destroyed ones.

According to an update CENTCOM provided to congressional national security committees, the military estimates the war has cost $43.6 billion as of Sept. 3. But that figure does not account for damage to U.S. bases and other military facilities in the region that were damaged or destroyed by Iranian strikes. The senators note that Cooper shared the damage estimate with the Senate Armed Services Committee in a recent classified briefing — and argue there is no reason to keep it secret.

"During a recent classified briefing, you provided information to the Senate Armed Services Committee on the estimated cost of the damage to U.S. bases and facilities," the senators wrote. "However, that figure should not be available only to lawmakers — the American people deserve to know the full cost of the Trump administration's unauthorized war against Iran."

The wreckage the estimate leaves out

The letter was led by Sen. Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts and signed by Sens. Mazie Hirono of Hawaii, Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut, Tammy Duckworth of Illinois and Gary Peters of Michigan.

A report from the Pentagon's own inspector general found that buildings and facilities at U.S. bases in Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Oman and Jordan were damaged or destroyed by Iranian strikes during the war. The most striking case cited is Naval Support Activity Bahrain, the Navy's main logistics hub in the Middle East, which was severely damaged by an Iranian missile and drone attack at the start of the war. Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao confirmed the extensive damage, saying in an interview that Iran "blew the hell out of Bahrain." The senators say the damage complicated the Navy's efforts to resupply the USS Abraham Lincoln, contributing to deteriorating conditions for sailors aboard the carrier, which was at sea for a record 286 days.

More than 50,000 U.S. service members are deployed in Central Command's area of responsibility. At least 21 have been killed and another 865 injured since the conflict began.

The military has justified not disclosing the damage estimate by saying it has not decided which bases and facilities it will rebuild. The senators rejected that logic outright: "There is no rationale for classifying that cost estimate. Our allies and our adversaries are, unfortunately, keenly aware of the extraordinary toll that this reckless war has taken on military readiness, and Congress and the public need this information to determine the steps necessary to recover from this debacle."

Why it matters

This is now the third front in the Iran war's cost fight. In September, a letter led by Sen. Elissa Slotkin and signed by 46 Democratic caucus members demanded a full breakdown of the $67 billion supplemental request the Pentagon says it needs. The Congressional Budget Office has put combat costs at $38 billion as of Aug. 1, with the future bill running $2 billion to $3 billion a month. Outside estimates run higher still.

The new angle — destroyed base infrastructure — is politically potent because it is about readiness, not just money. A wrecked logistics hub in Bahrain is a hole in the Navy's ability to fight the next war, and rebuilding it will compete with munitions replenishment, shipbuilding and everything else in an already-stretched budget. The Pentagon's excuse that it hasn't decided what to rebuild concedes the point: the bill is large enough that choices are being deferred. Watch for whether Cooper declassifies the number — and whether the Abraham Lincoln's record deployment becomes the emblem of a readiness debate that outlasts the war itself.
