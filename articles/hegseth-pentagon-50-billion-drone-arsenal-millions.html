Hegseth Says Pentagon Needs $50 Billion to Build Drone Arsenal by the Millions

Robert HayesOct 18, 2026 12:40 UTC 3 min readRegion: North America

WASHINGTON — Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said Wednesday the Pentagon will seek roughly $50 billion in one-time funding to take the U.S. military's stockpile of autonomous weapons from the thousands into the millions, describing it as the largest push to industrialize unmanned warfare the department has ever attempted.

Speaking on the Project Agincourt panel at the Association of the U.S. Army's annual meeting in Washington, Hegseth said the money would arrive through a budget reconciliation package rather than the Pentagon's regular annual request, framing it as a single investment in production capacity rather than a recurring expense.

"Ultimately we will be dealing with hundreds of thousands of one-way attack systems this year and into the millions in years to come at a pace that this department's never ever seen before," Hegseth said.

A second, separate budget track

The $50 billion request sits apart from the $53.6 billion the department already included for its Drone Dominance initiative in the fiscal 2027 budget request. That initiative divides its spending across five areas: $16.9 billion for unmanned system procurement, $14.4 billion for counter-drone defenses, $13.5 billion for contested logistics, $4.5 billion for collaborative autonomy and $4.3 billion for institutional systems and personnel.

Combined with money lawmakers already approved through an earlier reconciliation bill, total planned autonomy and robotics spending could exceed $55 billion, according to figures from the Federal News Network — though the new $50 billion tranche has not been approved and remains subject to congressional action.

Project Agincourt and the new command

The money is meant to feed two structures Hegseth unveiled in September: Project Agincourt, a year-long effort to test a faster way of buying autonomous systems that puts operators, engineers and start-ups in the same room, and its planned successor, the Autonomous Warfare Command, or AUTOWARCOM.

Hegseth announced the command at Marine Corps Base Quantico on September 30, saying it would hold "service-like authorities" under a four-star commander, with a target stand-up date of October 1, 2027, pending legislation from Congress. The Pentagon has already moved its Direct Reporting Portfolio Manager for autonomy into Project Agincourt, according to Breaking Defense, signaling the department is building the organization before the money is secured.

On the AUSA stage, Hegseth leaned on his own officials to strip away acquisition rules. "I want you to tell me which rules can be signed away," he said, pushing the department to open competition to newer firms and buy cheaper systems in volume rather than exquisite ones in small batches.

Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle said the Army is opening programs to non-traditional companies, widening access to test ranges and writing drones, autonomous ground vehicles, autonomous fires and autonomous lift into the way it plans to fight. The service has also created a robotics warrant officer track so skilled operators can stay in uniform for a full career, and separately outlined a plan to field roughly 340,000 small unmanned aircraft systems to combat units over two years.

Why it matters

The numbers here are the message. For three decades the Pentagon has treated drones as high-end assets built in small numbers by big primes; Hegseth's pitch inverts that, treating them as munitions — things to be made by the hundreds of thousands and consumed. That is the lesson of Ukraine, where cheap first-person-view drones have become the deadliest weapon on the battlefield.

The reconciliation route matters as much as the dollar figure. By placing the money outside the regular appropriations process, the department is betting it can stand up factories and supply chains in months rather than years. But reconciliation bills are political vehicles with uncertain futures, and Congress still has to pass this one. The budget language so far also blurs how much would buy drones outright versus funding factories, autonomy software and logistics.

What to watch next is execution: whether the reconciliation money materializes, whether AUTOWARCOM gets its legislation, and which companies actually get the orders. Hegseth's promise to let small firms compete with the primes will be measured in contracts, not panels.
